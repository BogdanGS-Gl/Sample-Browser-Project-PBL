Idea Background - explain why your research is important for you / domain (education, medicine, economics).
The first half of the research needed to realize this project helps us understand how sorting and searching algorithms generally work and how to use AI technology to provide accurate, relevant data when given user input. We're interested in pursuing this because it is strongly linked to Software Engineering and, given our curricular program, we believe these studies may increase our chances of survival in the later years.
The second half of the research involves building website models and UI elements, such as classified lists, search results and product descriptions, which are needed for turning algorithms into practical tools, as well as creating user-friendly experiences. For those of us interested in back-end technology, this is just necessary to study in order to properly plan and test program functionality. For the few of us with experience in designing websites, it helps us understand how to make appealing and informative graphical interfaces.
Research Question / Problem Statement - describe your problem statement and the question your research aims to answer.
Many ordinary people use music platforms as a way to quickly find pleasant-sounding music through automatically generated playlists and auto-played suggestions, because manually searching for new music involves research about artists and genres that usually takes long amounts of time and experience of listening to various records. People can miss out on art that they would otherwise remember for years to come, just because it's too time-consuming to find information online that would explain their appeal in a way they could understand.
The question we aim to answer is this - Can we make a system that makes it quick and easy for casual listeners to find good music that fits their preferences the closest?
Existing Solutions
Among the sources and platforms which serve the purpose of finding music include:
general music platforms for searching songs and browsing curated playlists(Spotify, Soundcloud, Bandcamp), AI playlist generators(ChatGPT's AI Playlist Maker, Spotify's AI Playlist Tool, Chosic AI), review magazines(The Rolling Stone, Billboard, Pitchfork), user-written reviewing and rating sites(AlbumOfTheYear, RateYourMusic) and independent reviewers(The Needle Drop).

** Project Purpose / Objectives -  The practical value your findings will provide. The problems your work can potentially help to fix. **
Our work consists of creating a website that helps people find favorite songs and albums faster. It does so through an algorithm that recommends the user a list of records matching their written prompt and creates a dedicated page for each respective record, displaying every relevant review, comment, rating or description found online that relates to the original prompt, all in a compact, easy-to-read space. This process makes it simple for music listeners looking to expand their palette, find music fitting of specific events and discover inspirational records they could remember fondly.

I have an idea to include in out project an AI - it is on everyone's lips, so it would likely be a plus if we described its use.

Automated track generation based on music theory and inspired by the Guangzhou fragrance museum: preferred musical elements—such as chords, progressions, tempos, rhythms, keys, and other characteristics—are identified from the user's favorite music, and generative AI is used to create the most suitable tracks for this person.

I’m no expert on the subject, but based on what I’ve read, music theory has already done the heavy lifting for us. It turns out that creating music—without aspiring to genius—is a trivial task. There are already off-the-shelf, highly customizable local models available (using specific parameters like chords, progressions, tempos, rhythms, keys, and the like)—for instance, musiclang-chord-v2-4k (https://huggingface.co/musiclang/musiclang-chord-v2-4k) or others.

The challenge lies in breaking down a specific piece of music into these characteristics and devising an algorithm that—based on the tracks listened to and their attributes—identifies the optimal characteristics for an "ideal" track. Since we have to solve the first part of the problem anyway (we certainly wouldn't want to manually tag thousands or tens of thousands of tracks), we can address both issues simultaneously; as for the second part, the choice of algorithm is limited only by our imagination—we could simply calculate the average across all attributes, find the mode, or even employ an LLM.

In any case, we’ll need to discuss this, and perhaps ask the mentor on Monday.

Streaming payouts are distributed pro-rata: a fixed share of revenue divided by each rightsholder's share of total streams (Spotify for Artists). A substantial share of listening is recommendation-guided, so a comparable share of that pool is allocated by placement rather than by deliberate choice. Spotify's own research finds that organic listening is almost always more diverse than algorithmic listening, and that users who grow more generalist move away from algorithmic listening (Anderson et al., WWW '20) — though the authors caution the relationship may not be causal. Problem: When one part of a song catches your interest, music apps can’t find more songs like that part. They only give you more of the same general sound.
Supports the problem
- Afchar et al. 2022, Explainability in Music Recommender Systems: users show “a substantial demand… to receive explanations for why certain items have been recommended to them,” and black-box systems make bad recommendations hard to understand.
- Last.fm API: track.getSimilar: similar tracks are “based on listening data,” with no reason given.
- Palumbo et al. 2025, Text2Tracks (Spotify): Spotify’s research on typed requests worked best when songs were identified from listening data rather than sound.
- YouTube Blog: Samples (2023): a feed of short clips with no explanation of why a clip is shown.
- Cao & Bogers 2024:
- 70% of music requests point at example songs, and 3.4% name a genre.
- Explanations were rare even in human answers.
- Human and Spotify recommendations for the same songs barely overlapped (0.000281).
- r/ifyoulikeblank had 944,629 subscribers in April 2023.
- Bhuiyan et al. 2024: “Not sure if there is a name for this style,” a poster pointing at “0:32 second,” and “Spotify is no longer capable of helping finding similar songs.”
- Lee et al. 2020: music similarity “depends on multiple simultaneous notions of similarity (i.e. genre, mood, instrument, tempo).”
- Flexer 2014: human judges’ similarity ratings correlated at only 0.37 to 0.43.
- Spotify Newsroom: DJ voice requests (2025): DJ handles “genre, mood, artist, or activity-related requests,” and none of Spotify’s examples describe a part of a song.

Narrows the claim
- Music Discovery Dialogue Generation (2024 preprint): people asked by artist (43%) and genre (17%), and by instruments, vocals or tempo about 1% each.
- MBW: SongDNA (2026) and Wikipedia: WhoSampled: Spotify already labels some connections between songs (samples, covers). The claim has to be about how songs sound alike.
- Cyanite: Similar song finder and Epidemic Sound: business tools already point at matching parts of songs. The claim has to be about listener-facing apps.
- Harris Poll 2021: about four in five US listeners favour music they’ve heard before, so the problem is occasional.
- Ithaca College and Cornell study: no popularity bias was found in Spotify, Amazon Music or YouTube Music recommendations.
Mixed: use with a caveat
- Millecamp et al. 2019: explanations of music recommendations raised some listeners’ confidence, lowered others’, and had no significant effect on trust (71 participants).
- User Experience with LLM-powered Music Recommendation, diary study (2025): explanations helped people put their taste into words, but trust dropped when an explanation didn’t match the song.
- Herlocker et al. 2000, in Jannach et al. ch. 6: the style of an explanation changed whether people followed a recommendation, and poorly designed explanations reduced it.
- Bannister & Eerola 2018: strong reactions to music come at specific moments of change, such as new harmonies, dynamic shifts and new instruments entering. This supports the focus on one part of a song, not the labelling itself.
- Baranes et al. 2026 (preprint): language models “capture explicit descriptors but struggle with context-dependent ones.”
- TechCrunch: Spotify Prompted Playlists (2025): Spotify now adds “descriptions and context so you know why” to these playlists. That confirms the need, but shows the gap is closing.

    We need to significantly narrow down our target user group. For over 90% of Spotify’s audience (which totals 777 million users), the platform already provides everything they need: it functions well as a player, its recommendation algorithms suit the vast majority of users, and its actual churn rate is among the lowest in the market—roughly 1.5–2% per month (our previously cited 24.3% figure, derived from Google Play reviews, turned out to be a classic case of survivorship bias). As for the remaining 10% of listeners who aren’t entirely satisfied with Spotify’s features, there is already a wealth of alternatives available. A need for granular control over recommendation algorithms exists only within a very small niche (well under 1%); most people who desire such control can already satisfy their needs by curating streams based on "vibes" or tags—using tools like VK Music, Yandex Music’s "My Wave," or Deezer’s "Flow Tuner"—or by discovering independent music for free via platforms like SoundCloud’s "First Fans" or Bandcamp.     "1 percent" is a significant exaggeration. In reality, the number of users who find existing search and recommendation algorithms inadequate is far, far smaller. We face a dilemma: in-depth manual curation of recommendations and support for non-commercial music appeal only to a very narrow niche (well under 1–2% of the market). If we were to build a traditional cloud-based streaming service—complete with our own servers, databases, and a heavy backend—for this handful of users, we would inevitably go bust due to the costs (this is precisely why the AcousticBrainz project shut down in 2022). Here is what we should actually do (this is not just a concept or a proposal):
    1) Honestly acknowledge the niche nature of the project (~1% "power users"): We must be clear with our mentor that we are not competing with Spotify for the mass market; rather, we are addressing the fragmentation problem faced by a specific, narrow group. This group might include, for instance, users who maintain personal offline music archives (FLAC files, Bandcamp downloads, soundtracks) or those who search for obscure indie music using acoustic tags—a niche that has been almost entirely hollowed out (especially following the split between Plexamp and TIDAL in October 2024; leaving aside the "vibe-based" search features found on platforms like VK, Yandex, or Deezer, this space is now largely empty). Even so, it is not certain that this group is small enough.
    2) Cut server costs to near zero (by adopting a "local-first" or client-side architecture): To ensure the project remains economically viable with a small user base—much like Poweramp or Symfonium—we should shift all resource-intensive computation from the servers to the user's own device. Furthermore, we can provide access to the Jamendo/FMA database as an open, legal sandbox for music discovery, without incurring the costs of hosting the music files ourselves. 3) Frame potential weaknesses as testable hypotheses (I’ve seen this done in another team’s proposal—they openly acknowledged that their intended approach had been tried before and failed, while also noting that many similar, successful projects already exist). Instead of promising an "industry killer," be transparent about risks—such as a limited music catalog or questions regarding monetization (subscriptions vs. one-time purchases/donations)—by including them in your validation and risk assessment sections.
    4) We either need to host the music ourselves or integrate with an API—though that carries the risk that the provider could cut off access (a trend that seems to be emerging). Alternatively, we could pursue a hybrid approach—doing both.

Problem statement nr. 1(Nikita)

    Problem: Over 80% of time on the start of making song producer spends on searching in libraries with more than 150 000 files of sounds.
    Modern music producers typically store between 500 GB and 3 TB of audio samples across multiple drives. Over 80% of these files lack accurate metadata or have nondescript filenames (such as Snare_02_processed_v3.wav). Existing desktop catalogers rely on manual tags, freeze when indexing libraries over 150,000 files, or require subscription-based cloud uploads.



Problem Statement nr. 2(Andrei)

    The algorithms of music streaming services (i.e Spotify) are repetitive and predictable, promoting familiarity over discoverability by often recommending the same known songs. This hurts both the users, who get fatigued from frequent listening repetition, as well as the small artists who have an extremely difficult time reaching an audience due to the algorithm promoting only the top performing artists.



Problem statement nr. 3(Bogdan)

    Finding samples in music takes too much time for independent producers. Independent hip-hop, electronic and experimental producers find it time-consuming to search for samples in existing records online, as most songs can only be searched by details of the whole record, like genre, style and artist, so they need to listen through them one by one until they find a part they can use.
    Context: Sources like Tracklib, Samplette and Splice exist for this purpose, with only Tracklib and Samplette offering categorization on published records. Tracklib only covers a limited library of licensed music, whereas Samplette's search tool is a random-suggestion button, but they don't provide information about individual sections of a song.



Problem Statement nr. 4(me)

    Active listeners who have used streaming services for more than 2–3 years (about 10–15% of the user base) find it easy to discover new music, but as a result, they end up with thousands—sometimes even tens of thousands—of tracks in a single, flat "Liked Songs" list, along with dozens of albums set aside for later. Consequently, 80–90% of their personal collection remains "buried." Platforms like Spotify, YouTube Music, and Yandex Music lack multidimensional filtering for one’s own library—Spotify, for instance, offers only basic, non-combinable genre "chips" at the top of the "Liked Songs" list, which disappear as soon as you start a search. Furthermore, hitting "shuffle" on a list of 10,000+ tracks causes the app to lag, often defaulting to playing only the most recent 50–100 songs. Albums saved for later are easily forgotten, as there is no system for tracking their status (e.g., "to listen to," "currently exploring," or "archived"). It is also impossible to quickly generate a dynamic selection from one’s own curated collection—such as instrumental, mid-tempo tracks perfect for coding or studying—without spending hours manually organizing everything into folders. Neither Google nor ChatGPT has access to a user’s personal library, meaning they cannot filter or play its contents; meanwhile, the once-popular third-party tool SmarterPlaylists ceased to function properly following changes to the Spotify API.

Problem:

    Active listeners (2–3+ years on streaming) accumulate 2,000–10,000+ tracks in a flat Liked Songs list and dozens of saved albums. As a result, 80–90% of their library gets buried: platforms lack multidimensional filtering, Shuffle on 10k+ tracks lags and loops the top 50–100 recent songs, and saved albums have no backlog tracking (To Listen / Exploring / Archived).



Target Audience & Sub-groups (10–15% of market = ~80–120M paid users globally per IFPI 2026):

    • Flat-List Hoarders (~45% of TG): dump all genres into Liked Songs, suffer from broken Shuffle and mood whiplash.
    • Focused Workers / Coders (~25% of TG): need dynamic slices of their own library (e.g., instrumental + mid-tempo for coding) because editorial playlists like "Deep Focus" are filled with generic Ghost Artist / PFC stock music.
    • Album Backloggers (~15% of TG): save 100–300 albums and forget 85% of them due to zero status tracking or notes.
    • Cross-Platform Listeners (~10–20% in our region): collection split across Spotify, YouTube Music, and SoundCloud.
    • Rule-Based Curators (~5% of TG): power users needing boolean logic (AND/OR/NOT, BPM, last played date).



Current Workarounds & Why They Fail:

    • Streaming Apps: Spotify genre chips can't be combined, ignore filters on shuffle, and aren't even available in Moldova; desktop BPM/Key columns can't be sorted or filtered. Apple Music still lacks Smart Playlists on iOS. YouTube Music has no genre filters for libraries. Only Yandex Music partially solves the worker case ("My Wave: Favorites + No Words"), but acts as a black-box radio and lacks Western major releases.
    • Google & LLMs (ChatGPT, Gemini): only do external discovery; they have zero access to a user's personal library, can't control playback, and hallucinate BPM/vocal status on 80% of non-mainstream tracks.
    • Specialized AI & Generators: Cyanite.ai is B2B-only, Sonoteller.ai caps free users at 10 tracks/month, and Endel/Mubert generate synthetic audio instead of filtering your own library.
    • 3rd-Party Organizers: SmarterPlaylists and Organize Your Music broke when Spotify deprecated Audio Features in Nov 2024. Chosic caps free analysis at 1,000 songs, while Skiley and Playlistation require paid subs and spam your sidebar with static playlists based on coarse artist genres. Marvis Pro (~$10) is iOS + Apple Music only. Record Club requires manual album logging.
    • Manual Sorting (Folders / Notion): sorting 3,600 tracks at 10 sec/track takes 10 hours of manual desktop drag-and-drop + 30 min/week maintenance.



PROS (Why it works & wins points):

    Operational, not informational problem: cannot be solved by Googling or prompting ChatGPT (directly passes our mentor's filter).
    Low direct competition in 2026: Spotify's API changes killed legacy free tools (SmarterPlaylists), leaving a clear market gap.
    Strong local angle: Spotify's basic Liked Songs genre filters aren't even rolled out in Moldova.
    Easy Midterm 2 validation: finding 15–20 university students with 2,000+ liked songs for video interviews is super easy.
    Buildable Core: DB schema, album backlog kanban, boolean query engine, true stratified shuffle, and LLM Text-to-Filter over tagged metadata are straightforward to build.


CONS (Risks & Challenges of solving it):

    "Vitamin vs. Painkiller" (Passive tolerance): 75–85% of listeners are passive, and even among the 10–15% hoarders, many just accept looping their top 50 tracks if onboarding takes too much effort.
    Hostile Platform APIs (Main Technical Hurdle): since Feb 2026, Spotify Dev Mode limits apps to 5 test users and blocks Audio Features. We'd need a client-side extension / export architecture and enrich audio tags (BPM, instrumental/vocal) via ISRC matching with MusicBrainz/Discogs, lyric checks via Genius API, and 30-sec preview analysis with Essentia.js.
    Partial platform patching: giants occasionally add minor features (e.g., Spotify saving single chip filters in Tier-1 markets).

Solution Proposal

A web-based discovery platform that interfaces with embedded streaming audio (via the YouTube IFrame API) to highlight, describe, and recommend specific sub-track intervals rather than full compositions.

The core user flow is as follows: The user loads a track (via curated recommendation, search, or random discovery). Through a Segmented Waveform Visualizer, the track timeline is broken into distinct, highlighted passages (typically 4–30 seconds in length). Clicking an active passage displays its descriptive tags (e.g., [Open Drum Break], [Rhodes Chords], [Dusty / Vinyl Texture], [Solo / Sparse]). The user selects the specific elements they want to explore. The platform returns a ranked feed of related musical passages across diverse catalog tracks that match those specific descriptors. Clicking a recommended result immediately jumps the built-in player to that exact timestamp and activates an automatic loop over the passage window.

This system eliminates the tedious, manual playback scrubbing and directly targets the creative moments producers actually want to manipulate, thus significantly accelerating the sampling workflow.
