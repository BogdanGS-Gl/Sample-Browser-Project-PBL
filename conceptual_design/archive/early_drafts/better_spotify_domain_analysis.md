# Better Spotify: Producer-Oriented Passage Discovery — Problem & Domain Analysis

## 1. Problem Description and Problem Analysis

### 1.1. Problem Description — Reference-Based Passage Discovery for Independent Producers

#### Who Has the Problem

This project focuses on independent hip-hop, electronic, and experimental music producers who use existing recordings as samples or production references. [Tracklib's 2024 survey of 1,107 music producers](https://www.musikindustrin.se/wp/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/AI-music-production-survey-2024.pdf) (recruited via social media and Instagram advertisements, with 94% male respondents) provides contextual information about the backgrounds of music producers, though it does not measure the frequency or severity of sample-search difficulties in this target group ([Tracklib, 2024](https://www.musikindustrin.se/wp/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/AI-music-production-survey-2024.pdf)). Within that survey sample, respondents describing themselves as full-time professionals represented 10%, while aspiring producers aiming to build a career (58%), hobbyists (25%), and beginners (8%) made up the larger share (totalling 101% due to rounding; [Tracklib, 2024](https://www.musikindustrin.se/wp/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/AI-music-production-survey-2024.pdf)). Separately, the [UK Musicians' Census of nearly 6,000 musicians](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6398a2cf26f9de4e45e94dd7/t/64fae3635773f313b194d963/1694163834806/MC23+Report+0923.pdf) found that 53% of respondents needed to sustain their music careers through income from sources outside music ([Help Musicians & Musicians' Union, 2023](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/6398a2cf26f9de4e45e94dd7/t/64fae3635773f313b194d963/1694163834806/MC23+Report+0923.pdf); [Complete Music Update, 2023](https://completemusicupdate.com/musicians-census-sets-out-the-financial-challenges-and-career-barriers-faced-by-music-makers/)). These findings make time constraints a relevant factor to investigate, though they do not establish how frequently sample-search difficulties occur among independent producers.

#### Why It Is a Problem

For producers who work with samples, listening to full-length music tracks frequently serves as the starting stage of sample searching ([Schloss, 2014](https://books.google.com/books/about/Making_Beats.html?id=1QwxBQAAQBAJ)). However, when a producer hears a passage in a recording and wants to find similar-sounding sample material across other full-length recordings, they face two connected challenges:

1. **Sound Discovery (The Vocabulary Gap):** A producer often hears a compelling short passage in a familiar track—such as a modulated synthesizer, a layered instrumental texture, or a distinct mix treatment ([Andersen & Knees, 2016](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2016/paper/000246.pdf))—and wants to use that passage as a reference to find other recordings with a similar sound. However, translating that audible texture into conventional text queries is difficult. Musicians frequently filter sounds using subjective timbral adjectives such as "dry," "wet," "bright," or "warm," or associate soundscapes with visual colors and aesthetic imagery ([Andersen & Knees, 2016](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2016/paper/000246.pdf)). In an experimental study comparing text descriptions and vocal imitation on a limited sound dataset, [Zhang, Zhang, and Duan (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.08661) noted that many computer-synthesized sounds "simply do not have semantically meaningful and commonly agreeable keywords." The study illustrates a limitation of text-based sound descriptions, though it does not directly evaluate reference-based search for similar passages in full-length music recordings ([Zhang, Zhang, & Duan, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.08661)).

2. **Sample Passage Discovery (The Whole-Track Bottleneck):** Even when a producer locates candidate recordings using genre, artist, key, or tempo filters, mainstream listening interfaces organize and return recordings primarily at the whole-track level. A producer, however, typically needs a short usable passage—such as an exposed four-bar instrumental break, an intro without drums, or a specific melodic phrase—and wants to find other full-length recordings containing passages acoustically similar to their chosen reference section. When relying on mainstream streaming interfaces without specialized segment-level search tools, producers may need to browse and audition candidate full-length recordings manually to determine whether suitable passages exist. For the initial concept, similarity refers to the audible characteristics of a selected musical passage as a whole; identifying an individual instrument or isolating its timbre within a complex mix would require additional analysis and is outside the initial scope.

This repeated checking may interrupt the creative workflow. Interviews with music producers at the Red Bull Music Academy have documented frustrations with browsing poorly organized sound collections—with participants noting that clicking and scrolling "takes for ever" and "ruins the flow" ([Andersen & Knees, 2016](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2016/paper/000246.pdf))—while research on music interfaces highlights the importance of a "rapid, iterative edit-audition cycle" during creative work ([Nash & Blackwell, 2012](https://www.nime.org/proceedings/2012/nime2012_217.pdf)) and a quantitative study of synthesizer users shows that adjusting parameters to achieve a desired sound is often viewed as time-consuming ([Kreković, 2019](https://smc2019.uma.es/articles/S4/S4_03_SMC2019_paper.pdf)). Information-seeking research similarly shows that search goals evolve dynamically as a user hears candidates, following an iterative "berrypicking" process ([Bates, 1989](https://www.emerald.com/oir/article/13/5/407/315058/The-design-of-browsing-and-berrypicking-techniques)). This suggests that reducing the time needed to audition candidate passages may help preserve a rapid creative feedback loop. Whether this benefit is meaningful in the specific context of full-track sample discovery remains to be tested.

At the same time, some producers value browsing and unexpected discoveries as part of the creative process ([VICE, 2014](https://www.vice.com/en/article/dusty-but-digital-how-producers-went-from-crate-digging-to-youtube-surfing/)), noting that "part of making music is about being lost a little bit and accidentally stumbling upon stuff" ([Andersen & Knees, 2016](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2016/paper/000246.pdf)), while others may find repeated listening and searching inefficient. The extent to which a more focused, reference-based passage search would benefit independent producers without diminishing creative discovery therefore needs to be investigated.

#### Stakeholders

* **Independent and part-time music producers:** Creators who search for reference passages and sample material within full-length recordings while balancing music production with other commitments.

* **Recording artists and rights holders:** Musicians, songwriters, publishers, and labels whose catalog recordings are discovered, referenced, or potentially licensed when a sample is cleared for release.

* **Music discovery and sample platforms:** Consumer streaming services (such as [Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/) and [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/)), sample marketplaces ([Splice](https://splice.com/) and [Tracklib](https://www.tracklib.com/)), and discovery utilities ([Samplette](https://samplette.io/)) that mediate user access to recordings and search tools.

* **Catalog metadata and audio-analysis providers:** Services and platform teams (such as [WhoSampled](https://www.whosampled.com/) and B2B audio-indexing providers like [Cyanite](https://cyanite.ai/)) that control how musical relationships, audio features, and track segments are indexed and exposed.

#### Why It Is Relevant

Sampling remains visible in mainstream chart music: Tracklib's analysis of the 2022 Billboard Hot 100 found that 17% of the year's hits sampled previously released songs ([Tracklib, 2022](https://www.tracklib.com/blog/state-of-sampling-2022)). Meanwhile, Luminate reported that audio streaming services hosted 253 million tracks at the end of 2025, an increase of 37.9 million year-on-year. Its report also gives an average of approximately 106,000 new ISRCs delivered to streaming services per day in 2025 ([Music Business Worldwide, 2026](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/quarter-of-a-billion-tracks-now-sit-on-music-streaming-services-where-does-it-end/)). Music streaming and video platforms are among the services producers may use to discover recordings and explore musical references ([Andersen & Knees, 2016](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2016/paper/000246.pdf); [VICE, 2014](https://www.vice.com/en/article/dusty-but-digital-how-producers-went-from-crate-digging-to-youtube-surfing/)), yet mainstream consumer streaming interfaces are not designed around locating specific sample passages within complete recordings.

### 1.2. Domain Analysis — Consumer Streaming Platforms, Sample Marketplaces, and Audio Search Technologies

#### How Existing Platforms Support Sound and Sample Discovery

Across the domain, consumer streaming services, producer marketplaces, and B2B search APIs support distinct parts of the discovery process:

* **Consumer Streaming Platforms ([Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/), [Deezer](https://www.deezer.com/), [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/)):** Streaming interfaces primarily organize music at the whole-track, album, genre, or mood level. On March 24, 2026, Spotify launched SongDNA in beta for Premium users ([Spotify Newsroom, 2026a](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2026-03-24/songdna-announcement-beta/)), powered by [WhoSampled](https://www.whosampled.com/) ([Spotify Newsroom, 2026b](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2026-04-20/deeper-music-features-about-the-song-dj-songdna/)). SongDNA enables users to explore creative connections behind a song, including collaborators, covers, interpolations, and known sample relationships ([Spotify Support, n.d.](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/songdna/)). However, its official scope focuses on surfacing documented connections between songs rather than performing open audio-similarity search to locate previously unlinked passages across the catalog.

* **Pre-Made Sample Marketplaces ([Splice](https://splice.com/)):** Splice—which reported nearly 350 million sound downloads in 2024 ([Music Business Worldwide, 2025](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/splice-which-hit-nearly-350m-downloads-in-2024-reveals-the-fastest-growing-genres-on-its-platform/))—provides advanced sound-search capabilities within its catalog of pre-made loops and one-shots. Alongside genre, instrument, key, and tempo filters, Splice offers machine-learning-based `Similar Sounds`, which finds samples with similar sonic characteristics ([Splice, 2022](https://splice.com/blog/introducing-similar-sounds/)), natural-language search via `Describe a Sound`, and audio-reference matching from a user's DAW via `Search with Sound` ([Splice Help Center, 2026a](https://support.splice.com/en/articles/13764370-how-to-use-describe-a-sound-in-the-splice-desktop-app), [2026b](https://support.splice.com/en/articles/13860589-how-to-use-search-with-sound-splice-sounds-plugin); [Splice, 2026](https://splice.com/blog/introducing-splice-sounds-plugin-beta/)). However, producers looking for material from existing full-length recordings may face a different search problem: identifying suitable passages within complete compositions rather than finding ready-made sample-pack sounds.

* **Sampling Catalogs and Discovery Utilities ([Tracklib](https://www.tracklib.com/), [Samplette](https://samplette.io/)):** [Tracklib](https://www.tracklib.com/) offers a catalog of more than 100,000 full recordings available for sampling under its licensing model, alongside a curated library of over 500,000 royalty-free one-shots and loops ([Tracklib Press, n.d.](https://www.tracklib.com/press)). Its tools also support working with and editing selected recordings, including the creation of loops and chops ([RouteNote, 2026](https://routenote.com/radar/tracklib-the-ultimate-platform-for-music-sampling/)). [Samplette](https://samplette.io/) supports exploratory music discovery through randomized playback of YouTube tracks, metadata filters (genre, style, key, tempo), and—in Samplette Pro—tools for retaining up to 1,000 previously discovered tracks along with notes and playlists ([MusicRadar, 2024](https://www.musicradar.com/news/samplette); [Samplette, n.d.](https://samplette.io/)). However, remembering or filtering a recording at the track level is different from finding other recordings that contain a passage acoustically similar to a selected reference section.

* **B2B Segment-Level Audio Search ([Cyanite](https://cyanite.ai/)):** In the B2B enterprise market, Cyanite Advanced Search ([Cyanite, 2025a](https://cyanite.ai/blog/advanced-search-api/); currently in Public Preview per [Cyanite, n.d.-a](https://api-docs.cyanite.ai/docs/advanced-search/)) demonstrates that segment-level audio similarity search is already technically implemented. Cyanite's API supports searching by reference track (including specifying a time interval within the reference track), free-text description ([Cyanite, n.d.-c](https://api-docs.cyanite.ai/docs/free-text-search-guide/)), and `Spotify ID` ([Cyanite, n.d.-b](https://api-docs.cyanite.ai/docs/similarity-search/)), returning both track-level similarity and segment-level match scores with timestamp boundaries ([Cyanite, 2026](https://cyanite.ai/blog/similar-song-finder-ai/)). Two distinctions frame our research hypothesis: first, Cyanite is a B2B API infrastructure designed for integration into third-party catalog systems rather than an end-user workflow built into Spotify for independent producers; second, according to Cyanite's documentation, while uploaded library files are analyzed in full, Cyanite's analysis of Spotify tracks relies on the standard 30-second preview snippet provided by Spotify when available ([Cyanite, 2025b](https://cyanite.ai/blog/music-analysis-api/); [Cyanite, 2024](https://cyanite.ai/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/CyaniteGettingStartedGuide120624.pdf)). Access to Spotify-specific analysis through Cyanite may also require the corresponding feature permission; the existence of the endpoint does not imply unrestricted availability to all API users ([Cyanite, n.d.-b](https://api-docs.cyanite.ai/docs/similarity-search/)). While these points reflect constraints of Cyanite's external Spotify integration rather than proving that no third-party tool could ever analyze longer audio, they highlight a potential advantage of a native Spotify feature—though whether a native feature could analyze full track durations across the catalog remains a hypothesized capability subject to technical and licensing assessment.

#### Platform API Constraints and Legal Boundaries

In November 2024, Spotify restricted third-party access for new apps to Web API endpoints including `Recommendations`, `Audio Features`, and `Audio Analysis` ([Spotify for Developers, 2024](https://developer.spotify.com/blog/2024-11-27-changes-to-the-web-api)). In July 2026, Spotify updated its Development Mode rules to allow up to 25 Client IDs sharing a developer-level quota bucket while retaining the February 2026 cap of five authorized users per app and the mandatory Premium requirement ([Spotify for Developers, 2026a](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/web-api/tutorials/february-2026-migration-guide), [2026b](https://developer.spotify.com/blog/2026-07-23-web-api-quota-updates)). In addition, [Spotify's Developer Policy (2025)](https://developer.spotify.com/policy) restricts unauthorized extraction of Spotify content, prohibits third-party applications from analyzing Spotify Content or the Spotify Service for any purpose, and forbids using Spotify Content to train or otherwise ingest it into machine-learning or AI models ([Spotify, 2025](https://developer.spotify.com/policy)). These public API restrictions and developer policies create major constraints for third-party applications attempting to analyze Spotify content externally. Therefore, the technical and legal feasibility of an external implementation must be distinguished from that of a native Spotify feature: a native implementation would avoid relying on ordinary third-party API access, although its internal audio-analysis capabilities, access to full-duration recordings, and licensing boundaries (separating in-app passage discovery and auditioning from audio extraction or sample clearance) remain hypothetical and would still need to be assessed.

### 1.3. Effect of the Problem on the Domain

Among the mainstream streaming interfaces examined in this analysis, the documented discovery workflows do not provide the same reference-based passage-search functionality available through specialized audio-search systems such as [Cyanite](https://cyanite.ai/) ([Cyanite, 2025a](https://cyanite.ai/blog/advanced-search-api/)). Current producer workflows may combine pre-made sample libraries such as [Splice](https://splice.com/) ([Splice, 2022](https://splice.com/blog/introducing-similar-sounds/)), licensed recording catalogs such as [Tracklib](https://www.tracklib.com/) ([Tracklib Press, n.d.](https://www.tracklib.com/press)), and broader discovery services such as [Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/) ([Spotify Newsroom, 2026a](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2026-03-24/songdna-announcement-beta/)), [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/), and [Samplette](https://samplette.io/) ([Samplette, n.d.](https://samplette.io/)). These services provide different search capabilities and address different stages of sample discovery. When relying on mainstream streaming interfaces without access to specialized segment-level search tools, producers may still need to audition recordings manually to determine whether they contain suitable passages.

### 1.4. Why the Problem Needs to Be Investigated

Because segment-level similarity technology already exists in B2B tools like Cyanite Advanced Search ([Cyanite, 2025a](https://cyanite.ai/blog/advanced-search-api/)) and sound-similarity search exists for isolated samples in Splice ([Splice, 2022](https://splice.com/blog/introducing-similar-sounds/); [Splice, 2026](https://splice.com/blog/introducing-splice-sounds-plugin-beta/)), the research goal is not to prove that audio similarity search is technologically novel. Instead, customer validation must test whether integrating a reference-based passage-discovery workflow into Spotify provides meaningful value to independent producers compared against their current baseline search process. While the hypotheses below outline the proposed measurement methods, full operationalization prior to testing requires defining what constitutes a valid acoustic match, specifying the exact baseline task protocol, and using a prototype or simulated workflow to test time savings in `H3`:

* **H1 — Frequency and Severity of Passage-Search Difficulties:** Independent producers frequently encounter difficulties when trying to find other full-length recordings containing a passage acoustically similar to a chosen reference. *Proposed validation method:* Semi-structured interviews and timed observation of baseline search tasks, recording how producers currently search, how much time they spend auditioning tracks, and under what conditions they abandon a search.

* **H2 — Sufficiency of Current Workflows:** Independent producers may find existing consumer discovery and sample-search tools (Spotify SongDNA, Splice, Tracklib, Samplette, YouTube) insufficient when their goal is to locate specific passages inside full-length recordings based on an audio reference. *Proposed validation method:* Observe participants attempting to find matching passages in full tracks using their current preferred tools and document where the workflow succeeds or breaks down.

* **H3 — Value of an Integrated Workflow vs. Creative Serendipity:** A producer-oriented workflow integrated into Spotify—where a user selects a short reference passage, receives candidate full-length tracks with timestamped segment highlights, and auditions or saves them in-app—improves search efficiency and user satisfaction compared to their baseline workflow without removing the sense of unexpected discovery. *Proposed validation method:* Using a functional prototype or simulated workflow, compare search completion time, perceived relevance and diversity of discovered passages, subjective preservation of serendipity, and willingness to adopt the integrated workflow against the baseline search process.

### 1.5. Problem Statement

> Independent music producers who sample existing recordings may struggle to find other full-length tracks containing passages acoustically similar to a chosen reference and to locate those passages efficiently, as mainstream streaming search is primarily organised around whole-track information rather than producer-oriented passage matching.
