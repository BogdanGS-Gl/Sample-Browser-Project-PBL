1.1 Music categorization online and how it differs among platforms  
   
The main actors we analysed fall into two groups: music platforms and review sites. Music platforms describe songs in very different levels of detail. Spotify shows few descriptive tags. The clearest are the hashtags on song previews in its scrolling discovery feed, which let listeners hop between feeds based on the sound they want (RouteNote, 2024), and since 2025 it has offered library filters by activity, mood and genre in seven markets (Spotify Newsroom, 2025). Instead of detailed tags, Spotify has invested in AI tools that take requests in plain language.   
Its DJ launched in beta in 2023 (Spotify Newsroom, 2023\) and has taken voice requests since 2025, so listeners can describe the vibe they want without leaving their session (Digital Music News, 2025). AI Playlist, launched in beta in 2024, builds playlists from typed prompts (Music Business Worldwide, 2024c). Both are limited to Premium subscribers in selected markets: DJ in about 60 countries and AI Playlist in over 40 (Digital Music News, 2025; RouteNote, 2025). These prompt-based tools have not made searching by fixed categories obsolete. They serve easy listening with minimal input, and reviewers point out their limits. One reviewer called "repeated songs and genres" one of DJ's biggest setbacks (TechRadar, 2025a). The same reviewer found that AI Playlist struggled to balance artists and eras: asking it for more '90s grunge "eradicated most of the modern tracks it started off with" (TechRadar, 2025b). 

Spotify's recommendations work differently from searching. Listeners are not asked to describe what they want in exact terms. Instead, the system learns from their activity across a period of time using the app. Spotify names searching, listening, skipping and saving to personal playlists as the actions that shape a listener's taste profile, noting when a listener is not engaging with a song (Spotify, n.d.). It also considers characteristics of the content, such as genre and release date (Spotify, n.d.) along with measured audio attributes for each track, such as energy, danceability and acousticness (which it stopped offering to new developer apps in 2024 (Krogsholm, n.d.; Spotify for Developers, 2024)). How these signals are weighted is not disclosed to the listener(such systems are often described as "black box algorithms”), leading to inconsistencies in how suggestions satisfy user wishes.   
More specifically, Spotify's Autoplay has been criticized by some casual listeners to "play similar songs so the music never stops" (Spotify Support, n.d.), and recommenders trained on what users already played can narrow their output over time. A simulation study showed that this feedback loop "homogenizes user behaviour without increasing utility" (Chaney et al., 2018), and in a recommender trained on Spotify's Million Playlist Dataset, "80% of all recommendations are concentrated among just 15% of tracks" (Baumann & Mendler-Dünner, 2024). 

Another layer of influence over song suggestions, besides audio data and individual activity, is collaborative filtering: a listener who likes a song is recommended what other listeners of that song go on to play. Spotify's algorithms consider trends and the general tastes and behaviours of users (Spotify, n.d.) and [Last.fm](http://Last.fm)(an online music discovery website), which suggests similar tracks "based on listening data" (Last.fm, n.d.-a).   
    
Other apps rely less on long-term personalization and more on immediate controls, which suit short-term goals and changes of mind. Instead of guessing what the listener wants, they let them pick the settings that fit the session. Deezer's Flow offers six moods, such as Chill, Focus and Melancholy (Deezer, 2021). Yandex Music lets listeners tune its "My Wave" stream by language, choosing Russian, foreign or "without words" (Habr, 2023; Rozetked, n.d.-a), and by "character", where an "Unfamiliar" setting shifts recommendations toward artists the listener does not know (Rozetked, n.d.-b). VK Music lets listeners combine several settings at once, such as a mood, new releases or music without words (Habr, 2025). Both also offer genre selections separately from these settings (Habr, 2025; Yandex Music Help, n.d.). Unlike Spotify's recommender, which adapts to listeners' behaviour, these controls take effect immediately. A listener who wants a change but is unsure what to ask for can simply pick a different setting. Options such as "without words" and "Unfamiliar" also address two common frictions of personalization directly: hearing vocals while trying to focus, and hearing only artists the listener already knows.  
   
Outside music apps, review and database sites such as Last.fm, RateYourMusic (RYM) and Album of the Year (AOTY) fill the gap in user control. They catalogue music on a large scale, independently of what streaming services license. They are also social spaces where small communities review records in their niches and draw attention to them. These sites let users sort and discover music by more precise descriptions than music apps offer: release date, listener counts(last.fm) and ratings(RateYourMusic and AlbumOfTheYear). For searching, RYM is the most detailed. Users navigate a taxonomy of categories(genres, descriptions, musical techniques, vocals etc.) whose entries are edited by users, cite outside sources and are approved by moderators, and they can tag releases as well as rate and review them. AOTY introduced a similar system in July 2026, letting users vote on descriptive tags called "Vibes" in the same way as genres (Album of the Year, 2026). These categories make RYM an accurate, flexible and customizable search tool. However, RYM is a database rather than a player, so it relies on users' own knowledge of music to navigate its categories. Even when a user knows which song to start from, a song's page lists only its genres, not the more specific tags that would narrow the search.  
   
To summarize, three models for finding new music, each suited to a different listening style: black-box recommendations based on long-term personalization, session controls with fixed but customizable settings, and precise tagging systems. Each balances user control against ease of use. For casual listeners of music platforms, categorization depends much more on the user’s own experiences than the music’s characteristics, which is instead more often used by fans of niche styles and artists to explore their preferences further than what can be provided by automatic curation, relying instead on written reviews and ratings to decide whether or not they should listen to a record.  
   
   
1.2 Listener goals when searching for music  
   
Before turning to how listeners look for new music, we examined which features on music platforms receive the highest complaints, to place the impact of unsatisfying suggestions and song repetition on users into perspective. Most complaints concern other parts of the platform: of 1,253 one- and two-star Google Play reviews of Spotify from 2024–2026, 54% mentioned ads, shuffle, skips or the free version, against 15% that mentioned recommendations (Google Play, 2026). An earlier study found the same pattern: only 27 of 1,000 Google Play reviews of five music services discussed recommendations at all (Baracskay et al., 2022). Listeners also tolerate imperfect suggestions. In a Spotify study of a weekly discovery playlist, 74.2% of users who loved at least one song were satisfied with that week's playlist, against 29.0% of those who loved none (Garcia-Gathright et al., 2018), although the study surveyed engaged users, so casual listeners may differ. When recommendations do miss, Spotify gives listeners ways out: they can exclude tracks from their taste profile, mark suggestions as "not interested" or snooze them for 30 days (Spotify, n.d.), and since September 2026, US Premium users can write notes to steer their Home feed (BetaNews, 2026). In general, recommendation errors rarely drive listeners' complaints, as long as they can still play the music they like.  
   
Surprisingly, music apps are not the most used sources for finding new songs. In a 2012 survey of music users, 82.8% consulted friends or family when looking for music (Lee et al., 2016), and in a diary study, most encounters with new songs came passively, through radio or TV (Cunningham et al., 2007). More recently, 84% of songs that entered the Billboard Global 200 in 2024 had gone viral on TikTok first (Music Week, 2025). In a Mexican study from 2016 on how different people search new music, out of 36 interviewees, several people expressed enjoyment in following recommendations wherever they led, starting "a chain of music" from one song (Ernesto, 19\) or "following suggested artists or similar artists to make fresh trails" (Chispa, 40). Learning a name for what they liked also changed what listeners looked for: once Elena (30) recognised that her favourites, such as Portishead and Hooverphonic, belonged to trip hop, she set out to explore the genre further through the app's radio (Ávila Torres & Beer, 2025). Another study centered around Spotify users found a similar variety: ten interviewees used the same discovery playlist for different goals, from playing "new music in the background" to treating it as "a springboard to find new artists or genres to explore more deeply" (Garcia-Gathright et al., 2018). Research on everyday music seeking adds that it is "often motivated by a vague or ill-defined need" (Laplante & Downie, 2011). Together, these accounts suggest that listening goals change as people learn more about what they hear and that finding music is a dynamic process, which isn’t always resolved from one single search result.  
   
Listeners also use different platforms for different kinds of discovery. In a 2022 test of five services, YouTube Music gave the most diverse recommendations and Spotify the least, with Spotify's suggestions "consistently less diverse than the input playlists themselves" (Baracskay et al., 2022). One likely reason is that YouTube also hosts uploads from anyone, not only music licensed from labels. Listeners appear to value this for discovery: in a 2025 MIDiA study, 52% named YouTube as a go-to source for finding new music, ahead of dedicated streaming services at 40% (We Rave You, 2025). Sites such as RateYourMusic, where users have left 176 million ratings (Wikipedia, 2026), likewise serve listeners who browse actively for new records rather than for music to play in the background.  
   
Overall, listeners' discovery habits fall between two poles: finding music to play in the background during other activities, which radio and Spotify serve well, and finding music through chance and curiosity, which YouTube and other open-upload platforms support. Neither group shows serious enough dissatisfaction with discovery to draw them away from their listening habits, since music platforms give them enough tools to vary their recommendations. In the case of listening fatigue, the dissatisfaction exists but rarely drives complaints or makes people switch.  
 

1.3 Artist goals when searching for music

1.3 Artist goals when searching for music

Producers search for music for analytical and practical reasons tied to their own work. They look for particular elements and sections of songs, which they either use as a reference for their production or sample, reusing a portion of the recording in their own tracks (Wikipedia, 2026b). The reasons for sampling a particular section range from its subjective qualities, such as a perceived mood or a pleasing combination of sounds, to its technical make-up, such as its instrumentation, effects and key. These reasons resemble the curiosity-driven, exploratory goals of the listeners described in 1.2, which suggests a link between how fans search for songs and how producers look for material online. Despite this taste for experimentation, time is a real constraint, since most producers make music alongside other work. In Tracklib's 2024 survey of 1,107 producers, only 10% produced music as their full-time job, 58% hoped to make it a career, and the rest were beginners or hobbyists (Music Business Worldwide, 2024a), and in the UK Musicians' Census, 53% of nearly 6,000 musicians relied on income from outside music (Help Musicians, 2023).

Sampling also works differently from everyday listening. Listeners find new songs gradually and are largely satisfied with what recommendation software gives them (see 1.2), whereas sampling means hearing large amounts of music regularly, saving material for later and drawing on several sources at once. Producers dig through YouTube, Discogs and AllMusic as well as record shops (VICE, 2014), and they keep large personal collections: one Red Bull producer described "two hundred gigabytes of \[samples\]" and trying "to keep some kind of organisation" (Andersen & Knees, 2016). Experienced producers build personal routines to cope. They create their own categorization schemes, such as colour-coding every track (Andersen & Knees, 2016), and search by criteria they already know, such as an artist's full catalogue, which can surface "the rarer stuff that a general search might not yield" (VICE, 2014).

Even so, the search stays slow at a professional level. In 33 interviews with emerging electronic producers at the Red Bull Music Academy, participants described browsing "really huge libraries" that are "not really well organized"; one said that clicking and scrolling "takes for ever", another that it "ruins the flow" (Andersen & Knees, 2016). Less experienced producers, without established routines, are likely to find organisation and time management harder still. The interviewees often searched for a sound they already imagined: "I am just waiting for the sound which I had in mind to suddenly appear," said one. They also described sounds in personal terms, such as a bass drum that is "round" and "dry", or a "bright" sound that "actually means a sound with a lot of treble", and some thought of sounds as colours: "Different sounds to me have specific colours" (Andersen & Knees, 2016). Hip-hop producers interviewed by VICE describe digging online as well as in record shops: on YouTube, "you can just type in something crazy and see what comes up," said Paul White (VICE, 2014).

Producers turn to full songs for two goals that pre-made snippets serve poorly. The first is to find parts that complement a sound they already have. Sample libraries now help with this inside their own catalogues: Sample Focus suggests sounds that "mix well together based on their tags, tempo, and key" (Sample Focus, 2016), and Splice's Create assembles "a stack of compatible sounds" (MusicTech, 2024), but nothing similar exists for published songs. The second goal is to work out what they want by listening. A full song presents a sound together with the music around it, while a snippet presents only the sound itself, so a producer who is unsure what to look for gets little help from tags. Listening through records is where much of this begins, since digging for material has long been part of the craft (Schloss, 2014), and what producers enjoy while listening points them toward what to sample.

Several things make this search slow. Full songs are described by details of the whole record, so producers listen through records one by one to find a usable section. Many sounds also resist words: in one study, many synthesizer sounds "simply do not have semantically meaningful and commonly agreeable keywords" (Zhang et al., 2019). Finding two sounds that are alike is harder still, because people disagree about what sounds similar; this disagreement even limits how well any system can measure similarity (Flexer, 2014). The search also changes as it goes, since what a person looks for shifts with each result (Bates, 1989), much like the "rapid, iterative edit-audition cycle" producers use in music software (Nash & Blackwell, 2012). When every step means listening to a whole song, that cycle slows down.

Not every producer sees the long search as a problem. For crate diggers, as one writer put it, "the pursuit for the crate digger was just as important as the reward" (VICE, 2014), and one of the Red Bull producers said that "part of making music is about being lost a little bit and accidentally stumbling upon stuff" (Andersen & Knees, 2016). For producers with little time, however, the same search competes with the hours they have for making music.

1.4 Producer-centric music searchers and recommenders, and how they categorize music

Producers rely on different services from listeners. Two of the most prominent are Splice, whose sounds were downloaded nearly 350 million times in 2024 (Music Business Worldwide, 2025), and Tracklib, which offers over 100,000 songs cleared for sampling (Music Business Worldwide, 2024b; RouteNote, 2026). Whereas Spotify and other music apps build their recommendations by learning from listening behaviour, these services categorize music by production-level values, such as key, tempo, instrument and sub-genre, and even by artistic influence.

Splice works with short, pre-made sounds. Users can filter them by genre, instrument, key (including major or minor) and tempo, choose between loops and one-shots, and search for presets for software instruments as well as audio samples (Splice, n.d.). Beyond filters, Splice offers two ways to search by sound itself: Similar Sounds finds matches "based on the similarity of its sonic signature", "regardless of its pack or its tags" (Splice, 2022b), and Create assembles "a stack of compatible sounds" around a musical idea (MusicTech, 2024). Artistic influence is part of the catalogue too: Splice's artist packs offer "our most coveted sounds from producers like jetsonmade, Travis Barker, Syd, Murda Beatz, and Ovy on the Drums" (Splice, 2022a).

Tracklib works with full songs instead. A 2022 review describes browsing its catalogue by genre and sub-genre, artist and record label, as well as through curated collections such as "Dark Dramatic Strings" (Attack Magazine, 2022). Once a song is playing, a loop tool divides it into sections of 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars, and some songs come with stems, the separate instrument tracks (Attack Magazine, 2022). Every track is cleared in advance: producers pay a subscription to download songs, then a one-time fee and a share of royalties to release a track that samples them (RouteNote, 2026). Since 2024, Tracklib has also offered royalty-free loops and one-shots in a library called Sounds (Music Business Worldwide, 2024b).

Smaller tools fill other gaps. The free site Samplette plays random YouTube songs filtered by genre, style, key and tempo, with a cap on view count so that users are "only served up obscure and unknown tracks" (MusicRadar, 2024), and Sample Focus suggests sounds that fit together (Sample Focus, 2016). Artistic influence also works as a category outside these services. Producers upload instrumentals named after the artist they evoke, such as "\[Artist\] Type Beat", a practice MusicRadar describes as less a genre than "a search engine optimisation tool for beatmakers" (MusicRadar, 2023).

Together, these services show a clear divide. Snippet libraries describe each sound precisely, by instrument, key and tempo, and can even match sounds by their audio. Full-song sources describe whole records, by genre, artist and label. None of them describes what happens in the individual sections of a published song: Tracklib's loop tool cuts songs by bar count, not by the sounds they contain, so finding a usable section still means listening.

1.5 AI searching and sample obscurity

The amount of music to search through keeps growing. Deezer reported in July 2026 that AI-generated tracks made up more than half of its daily uploads, about 90,000 a day in June 2026 (TechCrunch, 2026). For producers who value material few others have used, this makes the search harder. Obscure recordings are a prized find, which is why Samplette lets users cap the view count of the songs it serves (MusicRadar, 2024), and digging for rare records carries social significance in hip-hop (Schloss, 2014).

At the same time, producers are wary of AI. In Tracklib's 2024 survey, three quarters did not use AI, and 82.2% of those cited artistic reasons (Music Business Worldwide, 2024a). In its 2025 survey of 1,734 producers, 82% opposed generating whole songs from text prompts, although assistive tools such as stem separation were widely accepted (MusicTech, 2025). The objection is to AI replacing their work, not to help with it. One Red Bull producer compared a good search tool to "having a band mate", and another accepted it on one condition: "I would like the machine to sit in the chair, as long as I get to decide when it gets out" (Andersen & Knees, 2016).

AI is also limited at the task itself. In a 2025 benchmark of 10,000 audio clips covering speech, sounds and music, the best model answered only about 60% of questions correctly (Sakshi et al., 2025), and tests of music understanding found that models relied too much on the text of a question rather than the audio (Weck et al., 2024). Text search struggles in the same way, since many sounds lack agreed keywords (Zhang et al., 2019). The AI search tools producers do have work inside closed catalogues, such as Splice's Similar Sounds and Create (Splice, 2022b; MusicTech, 2024), and building similar tools on streaming data has become harder since Spotify closed its audio features and audio analysis to new apps (Spotify for Developers, 2024). A tool for finding samples therefore has to respect how producers feel about authenticity, which favours descriptions written by people over labels generated by AI, with AI kept in an assisting role under the producer's control.

1.6 Effect of the problem on the domain

Because search tools describe whole records, producers currently work around the problem in two ways: they leave it to chance, through random-discovery sites such as Samplette, or they use pre-made sounds from libraries such as Splice, which describe each sample in detail but only within their own catalogue. Neither describes the sections of published records, so finding one still costs listening time, and that time comes out of the limited hours most producers have (Music Business Worldwide, 2024a). Searching by genre or style also keeps results close to what a producer already knows, which makes it harder to experiment outside their stylistic boundaries, even though producers say they want surprise rather than "more of the same" (Andersen & Knees, 2016). As uploads keep growing (TechCrunch, 2026), the gap between how music is catalogued and how producers look for it is likely to widen unless sections of songs can be described in producers' own terms.
