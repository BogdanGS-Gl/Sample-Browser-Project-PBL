## Preliminaries

According to Startup Genome, 90% of all tech startups fail. Some of the more prevailing reasons for a startup to fail are: no market need caused by insufficient market research, being out-competed because of improper competitor analysis, or having an outright flawed business model. While not guaranteeing success, a proper Conceptual Design of a Software Application (PCAS) can greatly increase the chances of success by understanding whether a project is viable, even before actually implementing it [1, 2].

The Problem-Based Learning model (PBL) is often used for gaining knowledge about problems ranging from ones felt by a single human being to those influencing whole societies. This model is based on several essential design elements:
* It is important to **always challenge the problem or solution** that is being analyzed, as it helps teams detect when their project takes on a wrong turn.
* **Critique and revision** aims at enhancing the learning process, striving to always find the right problem definition or solution for their target groups.
* **Reflections** represent a tool to analyze data and make decisions based on how one feels about the project, the learning process, or the solution itself.

Many goals of PBL align well and are quite similar to the goals one has in the early stages of developing a startup [3]. As such, this project aims at providing students with an opportunity to learn in a PBL environment, while also developing skills needed to methodically approach any type of IT project such that it will (most likely) succeed.

## Getting Started

The project kick-starts with a pitching and team creation session. You will go through a process of initial idea validation to find groups of peers interested in a similar topic or idea.

Next time you meet with your team after pitching, you will learn who your university mentor is. It is important to connect with your mentor because no one will do that for you. Their task is to provide guidance and help you with questions. A good idea for the team leader is to interface with the mentor and create a favorable working environment. Also, the team leader should define communication channels between team members and the mentor, as well as find tools to manage the team's tasks. Be proactive and try to include every team member in the work process. Base any communication on respect.

After connecting with your mentor, your team will need to formulate a **Project Proposal**:
1. Describe your **Idea Background** and why this topic is important for you.
2. Define your **Research Question / Statement** — a short one-sentence description of the problem you will be working on.
3. Mention any **Existing Solutions** you are aware of.
4. Cover the **Purpose of the Project** — what value your findings will provide.

The project proposal is meant for your mentor to help you validate your idea and needs to be filled in before you can start working on the project itself.

## The Task: Midterm 1

For the first midterm you will be working on:
1. Crystallizing a Problem Description and performing Problem Analysis;
2. Proposing a Solution;
3. Defining a Target Group and carrying out Customer Validation;
4. Performing Comparative Analysis.

### 1. Problem Description and Problem Analysis
Before anything else, develop the problem statement from your project proposal into a complete **Problem Description**. A good problem description should mention:
* Who is this problem for?
* Why is it a problem?
* Which stakeholders are involved?
* Why is this problem relevant, and why is there a need to investigate this?

When doing **Problem Analysis**, consider all aspects of your problem. An important part is the influence of the problem on the domain it belongs to. As such, start by performing **Domain Analysis**, then write about the effect your problem has on it. Finally, formulate a **Problem Statement** — a one-sentence definition of the analyzed problem.

### 2. Proposing a Solution
After defining the problem statement, propose a **Solution**. This represents an initial vision of a system that solves the problem. Write about how your solution addresses the problem and which scenarios or parts of the problem it solves. Provide a scenario / user story describing how a potential user will interact with your system.

Next, create a **Value Proposition Canvas** [4] — a tool to ensure your proposed solution is positioned around your user's values and needs. Conclude this section with a one-sentence **Value Proposition** [5].

### 3. Target Group and Customer Validation
A target group represents a collection of people united by a common problem your solution aims to address. Analyze who your users are, segment them, and create **User Personas** [6] representing typical members of each target group.

Define **Hypotheses** about user behavior and pain points. Prepare **Questions** for customer interviews to test your hypotheses. Details about your Business Model or Funding Strategy may also be incorporated to show alignment with financial sustainability.

### 4. Comparative Analysis and SWOT
Juxtapose your proposed solution with existing competitor solutions. Create:
* A **Comparative Table** analyzing features of your solution versus existing competitors.
* A **SWOT Analysis** table analyzing your solution's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats in context.

## The Task: Midterm 2

For the second midterm you will be working on:
1. Creating an architectural concept of your application;
2. Connecting architecture with visual representations into User Flow Diagrams.

### 1. System Architecture
Describe your application from a systems architecture perspective:
* Create an **Architecture / Component Diagram** showing system components and their relationships.
* Describe each component in detail, explain how they interact, and justify why this architecture was chosen.

### 2. Visual Representations and User Flows
Create visual representations ranging from wireframes to high-fidelity mockups. Connect these visuals into **User Flow Diagrams** [7], representing the network through which a user navigates when moving through your application.

## The Task: Exam

For the exam you will be working on:
1. Redacting / Typesetting your Project Report;
2. Preparing each other for the oral exam.

After receiving feedback from Midterm 2, finalize your project report according to your mentor's instructions. Upload the final version to ELSE, print it out, and bring it to the exam.

The exam consists of an **individual interview with mentors**:
* Brush up on theoretical concepts learned during the project.
* Ensure every team member knows what other members did — doing only part of the project does not exempt anyone from answering questions about any part of the report.

## Reporting

Reporting is done via three required **Deliverables**:
1. **Project Report:** The core deliverable representing the whole semester of work. Must be typeset in **LaTeX** (e.g., using Overleaf) using the official university template. Consider using Grammarly to catch spelling, grammar, and phrasing issues.
2. **Midterm Presentations:** Aimed at familiarizing peers and the mentor committee with the report. Explain what you did, how, and why. Presentations must mimic the report structure and remain engaging and visually appealing within the allocated time.
3. **Meeting Notes:** Documented after each team meeting (with or without mentor). Used to evaluate teamwork efficiency. Undocumented effort cannot be appreciated or graded.

## Evaluation

The final grade is calculated as follows:

$$\text{Final Grade} = 20\% \times \text{Individual Midterm 1} + 20\% \times \text{Individual Midterm 2} + 20\% \times \text{Mentor Grade} + 40\% \times \text{Final Exam Grade}$$

* The **Mentor Grade** and **Final Exam Grade** are individual.
* Midterm presentations receive a **Team Grade**.
* Individual midterm grades are computed using peer corrections:

$$\text{Individual Midterm Grade} = \text{Team Midterm Grade} + \text{Individual Correction}$$

### Peer Correction Rules
For each midterm, the team submits **1 list of corrections** evaluating each member's contribution.
* Allowed values: `[-2.5, -2, -1.5, -1, -0.5, 0, +0.5, +1, +1.5, +2, +2.5]`
* **The sum of corrections across the team must always equal 0.**

#### Examples of Correction Lists:

| **Sum** | **0** | **Sum** | **0** |

#### Example Grade Calculation (Assuming Team Grade = 8):

| Team 1 Member | Calculation | Final Midterm Grade | Team 2 Member | Calculation | Final Midterm Grade |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :--- | :---: | :---: |

Corrections allow teams to reward members who made exceptional contributions or penalize those who failed to contribute.
